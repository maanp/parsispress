Clinical Documentation Layer

A documentation layer for outpatient clinics that drafts the note from the encounter itself and routes only the judgement calls back to the clinician.

Illustrative opportunity score

74 / 100

Demo heuristic

High-frequency pain with clear ROI, gated by regulatory weight and slow health-system sales.

The problem

Clinicians spend a meaningful share of every appointment typing what they just said, and the resulting note is often a poor record because it was written under time pressure at the end of the day.

Executive summary

Clinical Documentation Layer is a product concept for outpatient clinics and specialist practices. It addresses a problem that recurs often enough to be felt in operating cost rather than noticed as an inconvenience, and it becomes buildable because recent capability changes made the underlying work tractable.

The first version would be deliberately narrow — one workflow, one countable unit — with 6-8 weeks of work needed before any code is written to establish whether the problem is real. The illustrative score of 74/100 reflects medium confidence in the read, with the reasoning broken out dimension by dimension below.

Target customer profile

A 3-15 clinician practice with an EMR that generates a template but no real narrative. Documentation is the last thing done in the day, quality varies by how tired the clinician is, and billing queries trace back to incomplete notes.

Existing alternatives and competitor categories

  • Scribe services
  • EMR vendor dictation and note templates
  • General ambient AI scribes
  • Paper and post-visit charting

Most of these are adjacent rather than direct. That usually means the budget exists, the buyer tolerates the current state, and the real competitor is doing nothing at all.

Why the opportunity might matter now

The differentiating question in clinical documentation moved from 'can it hear' to 'can it be reviewed in under a minute and does it know when to stop'. Note quality failures, not transcription failures, are what pushed earlier ambient products into clinical governance processes.

Proposed solution

A documentation layer sitting beside the existing EMR: generate a structured note from the encounter, surface anything uncertain rather than guessing, and require explicit clinician confirmation before anything reaches the record or the claim.

Potential business model

Per-clinician monthly subscription with an enterprise tier for groups, structured around documentation time saved rather than ambient minutes billed.

MVP scope

  1. 01Encounter capture producing a structured draft note
  2. 02Explicit uncertainty flagging rather than silent completion
  3. 03Clinician confirmation gate before anything is written to the record
  4. 04Average documentation time per appointment as the core metric
  5. 05Specialty templates for three high-volume specialties

The scope above is deliberately one workflow with a countable unit. Anything broader belongs in the second version, if the first version works.

Key assumptions

  1. 01Clinicians will review rather than rewrite, which is the entire economic bet
  2. 02Integration with one EMR family is enough to start
  3. 03Note quality improvements are felt by clinicians, not just billing
  4. 04Governance and privacy requirements are satisfiable at a small company's scale

Each of these is a claim that could turn out to be false. They are listed so a founder knows what to attack first.

Risks and unknowns

  1. 01Patient data handling carries regulatory weight that is unforgiving
  2. 02Health systems have long procurement and security review cycles
  3. 03Established ambient vendors are further along on EMR integration
  4. 04A bad note is a clinical safety event, not a support ticket

Validation plan

  1. 01Measure review-and-edit time against the status quo, not against transcription alone
  2. 02Run a shadow note review with clinicians who grade output quality blind
  3. 03Start with one specialty where note structure is well defined
  4. 04Map privacy and security requirements before building anything that touches the EMR

Run this before writing code. If the interviews come back without a recurring incident, the honest outcome is to drop the idea rather than reframe it.

Three customer interview questions

  1. 1When your note is incomplete, what happens downstream — billing query, follow-up problem, or nothing?
  2. 2What makes you rewrite a note that was technically correct?
  3. 3What would a note have to look like for you to send it without reading it?

Suggested first experiments

  1. 01Shadow documentation for one clinician for a week and time it precisely
  2. 02Prototype with no EMR integration, on a post-visit transcript
  3. 03Compare a specialty template against a general template on review time
